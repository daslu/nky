Two roads, the receptors they arrive on, and how finely skin resolves what it feels.

Part one · the territory

There are two ways of being touched

The idea

Everything else here follows from one thing: touch is not a single sense. Two separate kinds of nerve fibre leave the skin at the same instant, travelling at very different speeds, and they are not carrying the same message.

The research This first stop is the shared framework rather than a single study — it is drawn from the review by McGlone, Wessberg & Olausson (2014)1, which is also the best single thing to read after this page.

The animation runs in real time. A fast signal crosses in a few hundredths of a second; the slow one takes about a second. That gap is the finding itself, not an effect added to illustrate it.

The fast road is the one most people mean by touch. Thick fibres wrapped in myelin, an insulating sheath that lets a signal race along at tens of metres per second. They are superb at what and where — the exact spot, the texture, the pressure, the edge of the object in your hand. This is the system that lets you find a coin in your pocket without looking.

The slow road is the surprise. Thin fibres with no insulation, a signal ambling along at about one metre per second — so slow that when someone strokes your forearm, that part of the message arrives something like a second behind the rest. They are poor at what and where. What they seem to carry, instead, is how it feels.

Those two colours — blue for the fast road, clay for the slow one — mean the same thing in every figure from here on. Nothing below assumes you have met any of this before.

For your hands If you have ever wondered why a client goes quiet a beat after your hands land rather than as they land, there is a real signal running about a second behind the obvious one.

You are working on a sense organ, not a covering

Where it happens

Skin is usually described as a barrier. It is also the largest sensory surface you have, and the fast road alone arrives on four different kinds of receptor, each answering to something different.

The research Standard anatomy rather than a finding2 — the classification of mechanoreceptors below is textbook material, and each type links to its Wikipedia entry if you want the detail.

Depths and shapes are schematic. Tap a receptor to read what it does.

Tap any receptor in the diagram. Two live near the surface and handle fine, quick things: Merkel cells for edges and texture, and Meissner corpuscles for slip — the receptor that tells you a glass is sliding out of your grip before you consciously notice.

Two sit deeper and handle slower, coarser things. Ruffini endings register stretch across the skin, part of how you know where your hand is without looking. Pacinian corpuscles register fast vibration, which is how you feel the texture of a surface through the end of a tool rather than through your fingertips.

And then the fifth kind: bare nerve endings with no capsule at all, wandering in the hairy skin. Those are the slow road, and the next four stops are about them.

For your hands When you change from a fingertip to a flat palm, from gliding to holding, from still to vibrating, you are not adjusting an intensity. You are moving between different receptors with different jobs.

Your fingertips are fifteen times sharper than your back

How fine

How close together can two points be before skin stops feeling them as two? The answer varies across the body by more than fifteen-fold — and the conclusion people usually draw from that is wider than the measurement allows.

The research The numbers everyone quotes come from Weinstein’s 1968 survey3. The test itself has been questioned for years as a measure of how finely you can feel4 — the ranking across the body is solid, the exact millimetres softer than they look.

Drawn larger than life so the smallest gaps stay visible; the proportions between body parts are the real ones. Values are the classic Weinstein figures, widely reproduced — the ranking is solid, the exact millimetres less so.

Pick a body part and look at the gap drawn to scale. On a fingertip the two dots have to come within two or three millimetres before they merge into one. On the back they can be nearly four centimetres apart and still feel like a single touch. This is two-point discrimination, and it tracks how densely the fast receptors are packed.

It is tempting to conclude from this that precision on a back is pointless. That would be over-reading a very narrow measurement, in at least three ways. Locating a single contact is generally more accurate than telling two apart, so a person can often say roughly where your hand is even where they could not resolve two touches. Pressure reaches past the skin — muscle, fascia and joints have their own sensory supply, and where you press changes what you are pressing on whatever the skin can resolve. And the precision may matter most to you: feeling your way is information travelling to you, the practitioner, and your fingertips resolve at two or three millimetres.

What the number does tell you is narrower and still worth having: do not expect fine surface detail on a back to be perceived as detail by the person receiving it. And treat the exact figures gently — people can sometimes report “two” when both points are in the same place, which suggests the task picks up cues besides spacing.

For your hands Try it on yourself with two pencil tips or a bent paperclip. Fingertip, forearm, back. Then have someone touch one spot on your back and point to where you think it was — you will usually be closer than the two-point figure would suggest.

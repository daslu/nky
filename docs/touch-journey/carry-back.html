What we would carry back into the room

The end of it

What we came away with, loosely ordered by how much weight we would put on each. Not instructions — a record of what the reading suggested.

The research Everything cited on the previous pages, weighed as part three weighs it. Nothing new is introduced here.

The figure from part four, repeated because it is the thing to leave with. Everything physical about the touch stays where it is; only what it is taken to mean changes.

  • Slower than feels natural — about a hand’s width a second.
    One to ten centimetres a second is where the research clusters, and it is slower than most of us work by default.
  • Warmth seems to matter about as much as speed.
    Around skin temperature — hands, oil, room. Cold shifted every speed toward less pleasant, and technique did not appear to make up for it.
  • Hairy skin looks like the home ground, though not the only ground.
    Forearm, back, shoulders. The palm has some of these fibres after all — just fewer.
  • Still contact seems to count.
    Resting a hand is not a lesser version of stroking. It may be preferred to stroking done wrong.
  • What you are trying to do decides which system you are working with.
    Fine detail comes through a fingertip on hairless skin. Contact and comfort come through a broad, slow, warm hand on hairy skin.
  • Averages describe populations, not the person on the table.
    The curve is an average across a lot of people. We would ask, watch and adjust rather than put a number on someone.
  • Saying what you are about to do matters more than we expected.
    You have access the social map normally saves for the people we are closest to. Saying it out loud seems to be how that stays safe rather than just allowed.
  • Describing the touch travels further than describing the mechanism.
    “Slow, warm, light, moving” is honest and checkable. “Activating your C-tactile fibres” goes beyond the evidence.
  • The hormone claims are the ones we would hold most loosely.
    That evidence is the thinnest thing here. The pain and comfort findings are steadier and need no help from it.
  • Most of the work is probably not being done by the fibres.
    Safety, consent, predictability and relationship. The fibres are the doorway, not the visit.

For your hands If someone leaves your room with one thing, we would want it to be the touch rather than the mechanism: slow, warm, unhurried, and from someone they agreed to be touched by. That is the part with the most behind it, and it is the part they will remember.

Every source, in the order the journey met them

The whole list

Every source the journey cites, and where we would send someone who wants to go further. Where a paper can be read without paying for it, the link goes to the free version.

The research Everything cited above, and two entries that are not cited anywhere: Morrison (2010) and Suvilehto (2023) are in the list because they are worth reading, not because a claim on these pages rests on them.

Four corrections the field has made to its own published work, kept in view on the way out. A literature that revises itself in the open is the reason the rest of this list is worth anything.

Where to go next, in the order we would send someone.

McGlone et al. (2014) — The standard overview, free to read, and the source most people are quoting second-hand. Long, but the first half is very approachable.

Morrison et al. (2010) — The friendliest conceptual framing of why any of this might matter.

Kidd et al. (2023) — The bridge from nerve fibres to stress physiology, and the closest thing to a review written for people who do this for a living.

Suvilehto et al. (2023) — The widest recent view — who touches whom, where, and what for.

Assembled in 2026 from the literature as it stood. A reading of published research, written for people who work with touch. It is not medical advice, it does not describe a technique, and it cannot tell you what the person in front of you wants. Where researchers disagree we have tried to say so rather than tidy it away — that is not hedging, it is the current state of a young field still arguing productively with itself.

Two · the second system

How the slow road was found, the patient who proved it carries an experience, and the two things it is tuned to.

Part two · the second system

Someone put a needle in a nerve and listened

1999

The second road was found by a group in Gothenburg who were not looking for it. The technique is microneurography, and it is exactly as unlikely as it sounds: a very fine electrode placed into a nerve in a fully awake person, listening to single fibres while their skin is touched.

The research Vallbo, Olausson & Wessberg (1999)1. Not disputed — this is the discovery, and it has held for twenty-five years.

The spike patterns are drawn to show the idea, not measured. The contrast is the real one: these fibres were told apart from pain fibres precisely by answering a gentle stimulus and not a sharp one.

Recording from forearm skin, they kept finding fibres that made no sense. They were unmyelinated — the thin, slow kind everyone associated with pain and temperature — but they responded happily to a soft brush, and hardly at all to anything sharp.

Of 38 units they picked for answering to gentle pressure on the skin, 27 were this low-threshold kind. That ratio is not a count of how common they are — the units were chosen for responding to light touch in the first place. What tells you these fibres are not rare is a separate observation: the team reported running into them about as often as the classic fast ones.

The title of the paper is the whole finding: unmyelinated fibres make up a second system for coding touch in human hairy skin. What it was doing there was completely open.

For your hands These fibres are called CT afferents. C is the slow kind with no insulating sheath, and T is for touch. That is the term you will meet everywhere else, and this is where it comes from.

The man who could not feel touch, and felt it anyway

2002

A second system is a nice idea, but how would you ever separate it from the first? The answer arrived in the form of a patient who had lost the fast road entirely.

The research Olausson and colleagues (2002)2. Strong evidence, and a single case — you cannot arrange for a second patient.

A rare nerve disease had destroyed his large, insulated fibres. On any standard test he had no sense of touch on his body: he could not feel a poke, could not say where he had been touched, could not feel vibration. The fast road was gone.

Then they stroked his forearm softly with a brush. He felt something — faint, hard to locate, and pleasant. And when they scanned his brain, the signal was not arriving at the usual place. It was lighting up the insula, a region that deals with how the body feels from the inside: hunger, warmth, breath, discomfort.

This is the study that turned an anatomical curiosity into a claim about experience. The slow road does not just exist. It goes somewhere different, and somewhere with more in common with emotion than with the map of your skin.

For your hands Affectionate touch is not a gentler version of informative touch. On this evidence it takes a different road and arrives somewhere else in the brain.

And it has a favourite speed

2009

If these fibres were built for being stroked, you would expect them to care about how fast. They do — and it is not the fastest, and it is not the slowest.

The research Löken and colleagues (2009)3. Widely replicated as a group average; individuals vary more than the tidy curve suggests, which part four returns to.

Curve shapes follow the published figure; the firing values are rough rather than exact. The dot moves at the real speed.

They brushed people’s forearms at five speeds while listening to single fibres, then asked how pleasant each stroke felt. The fast fibres did the sensible thing: the quicker the brush, the harder they fired. Speed in, speed out.

The slow fibres did something more interesting. They fired hardest at middling speeds, around one to ten centimetres a second, and less at both extremes. Drag the dial and watch the shape. It is a hill, not a ramp.

And how pleasant people rated it made the same hill. The speeds that lit up these fibres were the speeds people liked, and this range is often described as the speed of a caress. Worth a caveat that is easy to lose: when spontaneous stroking has actually been measured, it tends to run somewhat faster than the tuning peak, even though people stroke more slowly the closer they feel to the person. The hill is real. “This is exactly how humans naturally caress” is a tidier story than the measurements support.

For your hands The dot moves at the real speed. Watch it rather than the curve, then try matching it with your own hand on your forearm. That is what the research is describing.

It also wants you to be warm

2014

A second tuning, and the one most likely to change what you actually do. These fibres are fussy about temperature, and the temperature they want is skin.

The research Ackerley and colleagues (2014)4. Well supported, and a small study — as all microneurography is.

A simplified picture of two findings held together. The sweet spot is drawn as a region because that is how it behaves, not as a measured contour.

They repeated the stroking experiment at different temperatures. The fibres responded best to a brush at about 32 degrees — neutral skin temperature. Not warm. Not cool. The temperature of another person.

And the ratings followed. People found every speed more pleasant at skin temperature than at 18 degrees or at 42 degrees. Get the warmth wrong and the speed cannot rescue it.

Try the two together in the panel. The sweet spot is a small region, not a line, and both dimensions have to be right to land in it.

For your hands Cold hands are not merely unpleasant, they are the wrong stimulus. So is oil straight from a cool bottle, and so is a treatment room a couple of degrees under. This is the cheapest change on this page.

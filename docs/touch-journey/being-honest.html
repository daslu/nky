Part four · being honest about it

Four things this field has had to take back

2021–2023

Everything in part two is a decade old or more, and parts of it have been revised. A practitioner quoting the 2014 version is now quoting something the researchers have moved on from.

The research Six papers between 2021 and 2023, named on the cards: Watkins1, Case2, Ali3, Bendas4, Crucianelli5 and — the largest — Schirmer, Croy & Ackerley6, a review by researchers inside the field checking their own published work.

Four corrections from the last decade. Tap each card to turn it over.

None of these demolish the story. They complicate it in ways that mostly make it more useful, and they are worth knowing so that you are not the last person in the room still saying the old version.

The fourth is the largest and the least comfortable. Going through the literature, the authors found that of the papers using the phrase “affective touch”, only 34% even mention these fibres. A nerve fibre and a human experience had quietly become the same word, and they are not the same thing.

For your hands When you explain your work, describe the touch — slow, light, warm, moving, on a forearm — rather than claiming to activate a named nerve fibre. The first is accurate and checkable; the second goes further than anyone can currently support.

The fibre is not the feeling

Now

The most useful correction of all, and it comes from within the research rather than from its critics.

The research Sailer & Leknes (2022)7 and Schirmer, Croy & Schweinberger (2022)8. Position pieces rather than experiments — arguments about how to read the evidence, from people who generated much of it. Under them sit two studies that held the touch still and changed only what the person believed: Gazzola (2012)9 and Ellingsen (2013)10.

Both are small and both are awkward, and they are worth having anyway. Gazzola ran eighteen heterosexual men, and the man in the video behaved distantly where the woman behaved warmly — so belief about sex, about attractiveness and about manner all arrive together and cannot be pulled apart. Ellingsen’s effects on pleasantness are a fifth of a point, and its comparison session involved no spray at all, so nobody was in the dark about which session they were in. What survives in both is the thing we came for: the contact at the skin never changed, and what people felt did.

Two halves, and they are not the same kind of thing. Above the line it is illustrative — no study assigns those verdict words, and they are there because the point is hard to feel from a sentence and easy to feel from a switch. Below the line it is measured.

The same stroke, at the same speed and the same temperature, is not the same experience depending on who is doing it, whether you consented, what happened last time, and what you believe it is for. One paper’s title puts it plainly: meaning makes touch affective. The other argues that social touch is better understood as a tool people use to do something to each other than as a signal being transmitted.

This is more than an argument. In one study, eighteen men were stroked on the forearm by the same woman every time — she could not see which video was playing — while they were shown who was supposedly touching them. Believing it was a man, they rated it −2.53. Believing it was a woman, +3.05, on a scale running from −5 to +5. And the primary somatosensory cortex — the usual place, where the fast road arrives and the map of the skin is laid out — responded more strongly as well. Belief was not sitting on top of the sensation, interpreting it after the fact. It reached the place the signal arrives first.

The second study is quieter. People watched a short film about oxytocin, then sprayed salt water up their own noses believing it might be the hormone. Pleasant touch was rated a little better, painful heat a little less bad — and in the brain the two moved in opposite directions, sensory activity rising for the pleasure and falling for the pain. Whatever expectation is doing, it is not turning one volume knob down.

None of this is a demotion of the nerve fibres. It is a description of where they sit. They look like a doorway — a channel well suited to carrying this kind of contact. What comes through the doorway is decided by everything else.

Which is, if you think about it, what a good practitioner already knows. The biology has caught up with the room.

For your hands Speed and warmth are worth getting right, and they are the smaller half. Consent, predictability, and whether the person wanted to be touched by you are doing more work than any number on this page.

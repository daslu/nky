One · what pain is

How much back pain there is, why an experience is not a reading, and what happens to a signal on the way in.

Part one · what pain is

More people than any other complaint

2023

Low back pain is the largest single cause of disability on earth. Not the most dangerous, not the most expensive to treat — the one that takes the most life away from the most people.

The research The Global Burden of Disease study, 2021 cycle, published in 20231. Population studies from 204 countries, run through a Bayesian model to fill the gaps.

Strong for the ordering and the direction, softer for any single number — the authors say plainly that country-level primary data is thin and that they need better. The uncertainty intervals in the figure are theirs, not ours.

Counts, with the range the study itself gives. Turn the uncertainty off to see how much more confident a bare headline number looks than the evidence behind it.

In 2020 about 619 million people were living with low back pain, and the study projects 843 million by 2050. Both of those come with a range, and the range is wide enough to matter: the 2020 figure could be anywhere from 554 to 694 million.

The more interesting number is the one that went the other way. Over the same thirty years the age-standardised rate — roughly, one person’s chance of having back pain — fell by about 10%. More people have it and each person is slightly less likely to. The world got bigger and older, which is a different story from an epidemic, and worth having ready when someone repeats the epidemic version.

About 39% of the disability was attributed to occupational factors, smoking and high body mass together. Which leaves most of it not attributed to any of them.

For your hands The scale is worth knowing because it reframes the room. A person with back pain is not an unusual case with something rare going on. They are the most ordinary presentation in the world, and the base rate for “nothing sinister” is very high.

An experience, not a reading

2020

The field that studies pain spent two years arguing about how to define it, and the definition it landed on does not mention damage as a requirement.

The research Raja and colleagues, 20202 — the report of the International Association for the Study of Pain’s task force, fourteen people over two years, revising a definition that had stood since 1979.

Not an experiment, and it should not be quoted as one. It is the field writing down what it agrees on, which is a different kind of evidence and a useful one: it tells you what is no longer controversial.

The revised definition reads:

An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage.

Four words were added — or resembling that associated with — and they carry most of the change. Pain can occur with damage. It can occur with the threat of damage. And it can occur with neither, and still be pain, and still be exactly as unpleasant.

A diagram of that definition, not a measurement. The weights behind the switches are illustrative — no study assigns them, and the figure would be dishonest if it pretended otherwise. What is not illustrative is the shape: the signal on the left does not change.

Two words worth separating, because most confusion here is the two being used interchangeably. Nociception is the signalling — nerve endings responding to pressure, heat, or chemistry, and sending something up the cord. Pain is what a person experiences. They usually travel together. They are not the same thing, and either can happen without the other.

That is not a philosophical nicety. It is why the same injury hurts differently on a Tuesday morning and at two in the morning, why a footballer finishes the match, and why a person whose scan has not changed in three years can hurt more this month than last.

For your hands When someone asks why does it hurt so much if nothing is broken, the honest answer is that hurting a lot and being broken a lot are two different measurements, and the second one is not what the first one reports.

What happens on the way in

1965 · revised since

A signal from the body is not delivered intact. It arrives at a junction where other traffic is arriving too, and what continues upward depends on what else showed up.

The research Melzack & Wall, 19653 — gate control theory, one of the most influential papers in the field. And Mendell, 20144, a review by someone who worked on the original, setting out what survived of it and what did not.

Foundational, and much revised. The 1965 circuit diagram should not be presented as current detail — several of its specifics turned out to be wrong. That signals are modulated before they arrive is not in doubt at all.

Take the hand off the skin and watch the same pain signal get further. The gate is a simplification of a much-revised mechanism; the modulation it stands for is real.

Rubbing a banged shin is not a distraction technique and it is not a placebo. The large, fast fibres that carry touch reach the same junction in the spinal cord as the thin, slow fibres carrying the pain signal, and their arrival changes what is passed on. The hand goes to the shin before anyone decides to send it.

What 1965 got right was the principle: there is a control point, and the volume is set there rather than at the skin. What it got wrong was much of the wiring, and the field spent decades correcting it. Modern accounts also add something the original barely had — signals coming down from the brain that open and close the same gate, which is where expectation and attention get their hands on the dial.

For your hands A great deal of hands-on work is arriving at that junction with competing traffic. That is a real mechanism and worth saying out loud. It is also not a claim that you are releasing anything, correcting anything, or removing a cause.

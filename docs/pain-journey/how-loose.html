Two · how loose the link is

What a scan of a pain-free back actually shows, and what a coloured light does to a fixed physical stimulus.

Part two · how loose the link is

What a back looks like when nothing hurts

2015

Somebody gathered every study that had scanned people with no back pain at all, and counted how many of them had the findings that get blamed for back pain.

The research Brinjikji and colleagues, 20151. Thirty-three studies, 3110 people with no symptoms, thirty-two of the studies using MRI. Prevalence by decade comes from a model fitted across the studies, so these are estimates rather than counts.

Solid, and the single most useful thing on this page. Its limits are the authors’ own: many participants were volunteers rather than a random sample, agreement between radiologists reading the same scan is moderate at best, the studies span more than 25 years, and — this one matters — the findings were not sorted by severity. Pain-free people may simply have milder versions.

Drag the age. Every number is the percentage of people with no back pain who have that finding on a scan. The figure cannot tell you these never matter — only how ordinary they are in people who feel fine.

At twenty, more than a third of pain-free people already have disc degeneration. By sixty it is 88%, and by eighty, 96%. Disc bulges: 30% of pain-free twenty-year-olds, 84% of pain-free eighty-year-olds. Wear at the small joints behind the spine goes from 4% to 83% across the same span.

Put plainly: for most of these findings, having one is the normal condition of a human back of that age, and finding one on a scan of somebody in pain does not establish that it is the reason.

This is where the honest version has to hold two things at once, and it would be easy to drop one. These findings are not nothing — some of them, in some people, are genuinely part of the problem, and the authors say explicitly that imaging must be read against the patient in front of you. What the data rules out is the automatic inference: scan looks worn, therefore worn is why it hurts.

For your hands Somebody will show you a report with words like degeneration, bulge and wear on it, and they will be frightened. The figure above is a good thing to have seen, because it lets you say something true and calming at once: those words describe most backs of that age, including the ones that feel fine. It is not your place to reinterpret their imaging — but it is entirely reasonable to say that the words are more ordinary than they sound.

The same cold, two colours

2007

A very cold rod was put on people’s hands. The rod was identical every time. The only thing that changed was a coloured light, and how much it hurt changed with it.

The research Moseley & Arntz, 20072. A rod at −20 °C on one hand for half a second, paired with either a red light, described as hot and more tissue-damaging, or a blue one, described as cold and less damaging.

A well-designed laboratory experiment with a large effect, and a laboratory stimulus — half a second of cold on a hand is not a bad back. The abstract reports the differences between conditions rather than a score for each, and it reports them as approximate. It does not report how many people took part, so we do not state a number.

Switch the light. The rod does not change. The paper reports the gap between the two conditions rather than a score for each, so the pair is drawn around the middle of the scale — the distance between them is the measured thing.

With the red light, people rated the same freezing rod as roughly five and a half points hotter on an eleven-point scale. Not more unpleasant in general — hotter. The sensation itself reported differently.

They also rated it about three and a half points more unpleasant and about three points more intense. Those three numbers are separable on purpose: the study was designed to show that context does not simply turn one dial, it moves the sensory reading and the emotional reading somewhat independently.

The stimulus was cold. Believing it was hot and dangerous made it feel hot. That is not suggestibility or poor reporting — it is what the definition in part one describes, showing up in a measurement.

For your hands Everything a person believes about what is happening to their tissue is part of what they feel. When you name a finding, describe a technique, or explain what you think is going on, you are not adding commentary to a sensation that is already fixed. You are inside the thing that determines it.

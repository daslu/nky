What we would carry back into the room

The end of it

What we came away with. Not instructions — a record of what the reading suggested, in the order the page found them.

The research Everything cited on the previous pages, weighed as part three weighs it. Nothing new is introduced here.

The same judgement as the previous page, repeated because it is the thing to leave with.

  • Slower is the active ingredient, not deeper. Almost everything established on this page follows from rate. Very little of it follows from size of breath.

  • Six a minute is the rate with the most behind it. A ten-second breath. Heart rate variation is largest there, and the reason it is largest there is understood.

  • The best rate is a property of the person. Taller people sit slower, it does not move with mood, and it stayed the same across ten sessions. Six is a starting point, not a setting.

  • The blood is already nearly full of oxygen. Breathing harder cannot load much more on. What your breathing changes is how readily oxygen comes off again at the far end.

  • Carbon dioxide is not just waste. It is part of how oxygen gets handed over. Blow off too much and the blood holds on tighter and delivers less.

  • Light-headed and tingling means slow the rate. It is a normal response to over-breathing, not a sign that something is wrong with the person — and they need to be told that, or they will assume it is.

  • Breathwork reliably reduces self-reported stress, by a useful and modest amount. Twelve randomised trials. Not nothing, not transformative.

  • The claim with the most riding on it has the least under it. That breathing technique changes long-run health is what fills the rooms, and the trials for it are not there.

For your hands If a client leaves with one thing, we would want it to be a number and a reason: about ten seconds a breath, because that is where the heart and the blood vessels fall into step. It is smaller than what they may have been promised elsewhere, and they can check it on their own wrist.

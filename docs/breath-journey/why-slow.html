Two · why slow works

The heart is already following your breath, one rate makes that swing largest, and that rate is not the same for everybody.

Part two · why slow works

The heart is already breathing with you

Now, in you

Your heart rate is not steady. It rises as you breathe in and falls as you breathe out, and it is doing that at this moment.

The research Lehrer & Gevirtz, 20141, and Russo and colleagues, 20172. The pattern has a name — respiratory sinus arrhythmia — and the 2014 review states it plainly: heart rate “increases during inhalation and decreases during exhalation”.

As solid as anything on these pages. It is directly observable in anyone, with any device that reads a pulse, and it needs no statistics at all.

Move through one breath. The direction is the established finding; the size of the swing drawn here is illustrative, because yours depends on your age, your fitness and how slowly you are breathing.

If you have ever put a pulse oximeter on someone and watched the number wander, this is what you were watching. It is not the device being imprecise.

Why it happens is a longer story than it looks, and the 2014 review is careful to say that parts of the account are proposed rather than settled. The short version is that the same brainstem machinery times the breath and adjusts the heart, and that the pressure sensors in the big arteries — the baroreflex — are correcting blood pressure continuously as the chest changes shape around them.

The useful part for a practitioner is simpler. The breath is not a separate system you are reaching in to influence. It is already wired to the heart, continuously, in everyone, and what a breathing instruction does is change the timing of something that is happening anyway.

For your hands This is the one claim on the page a client can check for themselves in thirty seconds with a finger on their own wrist and a long slow breath. Things people verify on their own body land differently from things they are told.

One rate rings louder than the others

2014

Breathe at about six a minute and the swing in your heart rate gets much bigger. Not gradually bigger as you slow down — biggest at one particular rate, and smaller again either side of it.

The research Lehrer & Gevirtz, 20141. “Maximum heart rate oscillations at respiratory frequency occurred at approximately 0.1 Hz (six breaths per minute)”, refined in the same review to about 5.5 a minute, a breath of roughly eleven seconds. Russo’s review reports the same peak2.

Where the peak sits is replicated and solid. The mechanism has a good account — and the authors of the review are explicit that parts of it are speculation, including the newer suggestion about signals travelling up the vagus nerve to the front of the brain.

Find the peak. Where it sits is the established finding; the height of the hill is drawn rather than measured, and how sharp yours is will not be quite this.

Here is the part that makes it a resonance rather than just a good rate. Your blood pressure is being corrected continuously by the baroreflex, and that correction takes a few seconds to arrive. Breathe at around six a minute and each breath’s push lands just as the correction from the last one comes through — heart rate and blood pressure end up running half a cycle apart, blood pressure falling as heart rate rises. The timing is what does it: every push adds to the swing already there instead of fighting it.

It is the same reason a child on a swing goes higher when the pushes are timed to the swing rather than delivered faster. The system has a natural period, and matching it costs nothing and does more.

What this does not establish is what people usually go on to say. A bigger swing in heart rate is a real physiological change, and it is a measurement, not an outcome. The step from this makes your heart rate variation larger to this makes you healthier is the step part three is about, and it is much less secure than this stop.

For your hands If you are going to give someone a number, six a minute is the one with the most behind it — in through the nose for about four seconds, out for about six, and no straining at either end. Longer out than in is the part most people find easiest to hold on to.

And it is not the same rate for everybody

2006

Six a minute is the average of a spread, not a setting. People have their own rate, it can be found, and it stays put.

The research Vaschillo, Vaschillo & Lehrer, 20063 — 32 adults with asthma and 24 healthy adults, each tested to find the rate at which their own heart rate swung most.

One laboratory, 56 people, and the findings are reported as directions rather than as numbers — which is why the figure shows directions and no numbers. Good enough to say the spread is real and roughly what predicts it. Not good enough to give anyone a formula.

What predicted a person’s own best rate, and what did not. No numbers on this figure, deliberately — the paper does not publish the coefficients and inventing them would be worse than leaving them out.

Taller people resonated at a slower rate, and men slower than women — which is very likely the same finding twice, since the paper relates it to blood volume rather than to sex as such. Age did not predict it. Weight did not. Having asthma did not.

And the finding that matters most for anyone teaching this: a person’s rate stayed the same across ten sessions. It is a stable property of them, like a resting heart rate, rather than something that moves with their mood or how the day has gone.

So the honest version of “breathe at six a minute” is: six is a good place to start, your own best rate is probably within a breath or two either side of it, and the way to find it is to try a few and notice which one feels like it takes the least effort to keep going.

For your hands If six feels wrong for someone — strained, or oddly effortful — that is worth taking at face value rather than as a failure to relax. Try five and try seven. Bigger people tend to sit at the slower end.

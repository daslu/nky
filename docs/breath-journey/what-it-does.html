One · what breathing is doing

What an ordinary rate looks like, why the lungs are not the interesting part, and what goes wrong when you breathe more than the body asked for.

Part one · what breathing is doing

What an ordinary rate looks like

2017

“Slow breathing” sounds like it must begin somewhere unusual. It begins one breath below where most people already are.

The research Russo, Santarelli & O’Rourke, 20171 — a review of the physiology of slow breathing in healthy people, written for a respiratory audience and free to read.

A review rather than an experiment, and the best single overview of this material. Its authors end by saying there is “a dire need for further research into the area”, which is worth carrying through everything below.

Drag the rate. The same number is shown as a breath length underneath, because seconds per breath is what anyone actually counts.

The review puts an ordinary resting rate at 10 to 20 breaths a minute, and defines slow breathing as 4 to 10. The two meet exactly at ten, which is the useful thing to notice: there is no gap between normal and slow, and nothing exotic is being asked for.

At six breaths a minute — the rate the rest of this page is about — a breath takes ten seconds. That is long enough to feel deliberate and short enough that most people can hold it without strain. Below about four a minute the research thins out, which is why the figure stops naming the bands there.

For your hands “Breathe slowly” is not a useful instruction, because it has no number in it. “About ten seconds a breath” is one, and someone can check themselves against it.

The lungs are not where the interesting part is

Standard physiology

Breathing harder cannot load much more oxygen onto the blood, because the blood is very nearly full already. What changes with your breathing is how readily the oxygen comes off again at the far end.

The research The oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve2 — textbook respiratory physiology, with no single modern source to point at, so the reference is a pointer to a standard description rather than to a study. Nothing here is contested.

The figure draws the curve from the standard equation. It is a shape, not measured data, and it is a good shape: it has been the accepted description for over a century.

Move the carbon dioxide. Watch the gap between the two dots — that gap is what actually gets handed over to the tissue, and the low end of the curve is the end that moves.

Two things are worth separating here, and almost every workshop account runs them together.

Loading. Blood leaving healthy lungs is already carrying oxygen at something like 97 or 98% of what it can hold. The top of that curve is nearly flat, so breathing harder pushes you along the flat part. There is very little headroom, which is why getting more oxygen in is not the mechanism for anything you are about to feel.

Unloading. The bottom of the curve is steep, and that is the end that responds to carbon dioxide. More carbon dioxide, and a slightly more acid blood, makes haemoglobin loosen its grip and hand oxygen over more readily. This is the Bohr effect, and it points the opposite way to most people’s intuition: carbon dioxide is not simply waste to be got rid of. It is part of how the delivery works.

So blowing off a lot of carbon dioxide shifts the whole curve leftward, and the blood holds on tighter. Oxygen-rich blood arrives at the tissue and hands over less of its load. More breathing, less delivered.

There is a real benefit at the other end of this, and the review reports it: breathing slowly at around six a minute produced a small rise in blood oxygen saturation, by using the lungs more efficiently rather than by moving more air. Slow and efficient, not fast and hard.

For your hands If someone on your table starts breathing big and fast because they were told deep breathing is good, that is not a mild version of the right thing. Slower is the ingredient. Bigger, on its own, is the next stop.

When more breathing is less

2005

The light-headed, tingling, faintly unreal feeling that comes with over-breathing is not anxiety about the exercise. It is a measurable drop in blood flow to the brain.

The research Stocchetti and colleagues, 20053, a review of deliberately induced hyperventilation in head injury.

An odd-looking source for a page like this, and the honest one. In intensive care, hyperventilation is used on purpose to narrow blood vessels in the brain and lower the pressure inside the skull — and its known danger is cutting blood flow too far. That establishes the direction, and that the lever is large. It says nothing about the dose anyone gets on a mat, and the figure carries no dose for that reason.

Directions only, no numbers. The chain is well established; how far along it any particular person travels is not something anyone can currently put a figure on.

The chain runs: breathe more than the body is asking for → carbon dioxide in the blood falls → blood vessels in the brain narrow → less blood reaches the brain → light-headed, tingling hands and lips, a sense of unreality. Add the Bohr effect from the last stop and the blood that does arrive gives up less of its oxygen.

None of that is dangerous in a healthy person doing it briefly, and it stops when the breathing stops. It is worth knowing anyway, for two reasons. It explains a thing you will see. And it is the clearest case on this page where more of the good thing is simply a different thing.

Note what the honest version cannot say. It cannot tell you how fast is too fast, because the evidence comes from a setting where the amount is controlled by machine and measured directly. Anyone offering a threshold is going past their evidence.

For your hands Tingling hands and light-headedness in a breathing exercise are a signal to slow the rate, not to push through. The nearest thing to an antidote is an unhurried, ordinary breath — and the person needs to be told it is a normal response to over-breathing, or they will read it as something being wrong with them.
